\begin{abstract}
\noindent
4chan's /pol/ is routinely described as an \emph{alternative media ecosystem}:
a space where legacy outlets are rejected and a partisan counter-canon takes
their place. The claim is widely repeated and rarely measured. We measure it
over 24 daily captures spanning 45 days (2026-07-22 to 2026-09-04): 6{,}532
threads, 278{,}706 posts, and 10{,}078 citation events across 1{,}408 unique
domains, each domain coded against a published codebook.

Three findings work against the framing. First, news outlets of any kind
occupy \textbf{12.8\%} of citation events; social platforms take 43.7\% and
archives and file hosts 11.0\%, the latter cited more often than any single
news category. Second, sourcing is extremely concentrated, with a Gini
coefficient of $0.821$, YouTube alone supplying 28.9\% of citations, twelve
domains supplying half, and
70.2\% of domains appearing exactly once. Third, and most directly, there is no
alternative-media cluster in the co-citation structure. Within the news
subgraph, assortativity by orientation is $-0.042$; alternative outlets show no
tendency to co-occur with one another (1 observed edge against 0.81 expected
under a degree-preserving null, $p=0.62$) and are peripheral (mean degree $0.9$
against $2.2$ for mainstream). Communities partition by topic and platform
affordance, not by editorial orientation.

We report two methodological results alongside. Curated source lists derived
from US-centric rating databases undercounted news on this corpus by a factor
of 2.8, systematically dropping British, Australian, Israeli and Ukrainian
outlets from a corpus dominated by threads about wars outside the United
States. And repeated captures of the same threads put a measured floor on
survivorship bias: 0.68\% of posts (95\% CI [0.44, 1.07]) disappear between
observations.

We deliberately exclude sentiment analysis. A transformer scored every cited
post, but the measure could not be validated on this domain and we judged it
unfit to carry a conclusion; Section~\ref{sec:nosentiment} states the reasoning
in full.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

4chan's /pol/ (``Politically Incorrect'') board occupies an outsized place in
accounts of online radicalisation and information disorder. Measurement work has
characterised its language, its raiding behaviour, and its position in the wider
web ecosystem \citep{hine2017,papasavva2020}. A recurring assumption in that
literature, and in journalism drawing on it, is that /pol/ constitutes an
alternative media ecosystem, with a counter-canon of partisan publishers
displacing the legacy press.

Testing the claim requires knowing what /pol/ cites, in what proportion, and
whether its sources organise along a partisan axis. This paper supplies that
measurement for a bounded window, and makes five contributions. The first is a
\textbf{citation-level audit} of 10{,}078 citation events, with proportions
reported as Wilson score intervals, which places news of any kind at 12.8\% of
the total. The second is a \textbf{concentration analysis} quantifying how
narrow the resulting diet is. The third is a \textbf{structural test} of the
counter-canon claim, measuring co-citation assortativity by editorial
orientation, which finds none. The fourth is a \textbf{published codebook} of
203 manually coded domains, together with evidence that US-centric source
taxonomies undercount news on this corpus by a factor of 2.8. The fifth is a
\textbf{measured floor on survivorship bias}, derived from repeated captures of
the same threads.

\section{Theoretical framework and prior literature}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Fringe platforms as objects of political measurement}

Research on /pol/ began as a measurement problem. A corpus of roughly 8M posts
gathered over two and a half months yielded the first systematic
characterisation of the board, covering posting dynamics, the prevalence of hate
speech (12\% of /pol/ posts contained a term from a crowdsourced hate lexicon,
against 2.2\% of a comparison sample of tweets), and the board's role in
coordinating harassment campaigns against other platforms \citep{hine2017}. The
empirical base was later extended to 134.5M posts across 3.5 years, with
toxicity scores and named-entity annotations attached and dataset conventions
established that much subsequent work inherits \citep{papasavva2020}. That
corpus places 37\% of posts above a Perspective \textsc{toxicity} score of $0.5$
and puts the average thread at 39.6 posts.

Both studies treat the board as politically significant for what it produces,
meaning memes, vocabulary and coordinated aggression, and for how it behaves,
meaning velocity, ephemerality and raiding. Neither adopts the citation event as
a unit of analysis, and neither reports category-level proportions for the
external sources the board circulates. That gap matters, because claims made
about /pol/ in political communication typically concern its information supply
rather than its output.

The 2016 measurement comes closest \citep{hine2017}. Its media analysis ranks
external domains posted to /pol/ and finds YouTube an order of magnitude ahead
of the next two sites, Wikipedia and Twitter, followed by Archive.is, Wikileaks,
pastebin and DonaldJTrump.com, with the Daily Mail and Breitbart the first news
outlets to appear. That study reads the absence of CNN, the BBC and the Guardian
from the leading ranks as an indication of how polarised opinion on the board
has become. That
inference rests on rank position within an undifferentiated domain list, a
quantity sensitive to how many non-news domains crowd the upper ranks, and it
leaves the share of citations going to established outlets distinct from the
rank any single outlet occupies. Our design measures the same underlying
structure at the level of source categories. Nearly a decade separates the two
observation windows, so the comparison we draw in Section~\ref{sec:network}
complements their reading rather than displacing it.

\subsection{The alternative media ecosystem thesis}

The claim this paper tests is broader than any single study. Fringe political
communities are widely held to have constructed an alternative epistemic
infrastructure, a counter-canon of partisan publishers that displaces the
established press and supplies the community with its own account of events.
The thesis has an appealing internal logic. A community hostile to professional
journalism should be expected to substitute something for it, and the outlets
named in that substitution are usually the ones whose partisan alignment matches
the community's.

Stated carefully, the thesis carries three observable implications for citation
behaviour. Alternative outlets should be numerically substantial within the
community's information supply. They should travel together, appearing in the
same discussions as one another rather than alongside the established press. And
they should be structurally central, functioning as reference points that other
sources cluster around. Each implication is measurable from citation data alone,
without recourse to any model of what participants think.

\subsection{Circulation, platforms and the archive}

A parallel literature treats fringe communities as nodes in a cross-platform
circulation system. The propagation of images and links between fringe
communities such as 4chan and Gab and mainstream networks such as Twitter and
Reddit has been traced in detail, establishing that the relevant object of study
is the movement of artefacts across platform boundaries rather than the
editorial content of any one source \citep{zannettou2018memes}. A companion
survey of rumours, hoaxes and unreliable sourcing within online communities
situates source-sharing as one observable trace of disinformation dynamics
\citep{zannettou2019web}.

This literature suggests a rival account of what organises a fringe community's
information supply. Circulation may follow platform affordance, meaning what a
given host makes easy to embed, mirror or preserve, together with the practical
demands of a subject matter, in place of the editorial provenance of sources.
Two features of /pol/ make the rival account plausible in advance. Ephemerality
is designed into the medium, since threads fall off the catalogue and are
permanently deleted after seven days \citep{papasavva2020}, which gives
participants a standing incentive to mirror and archive. And the board's
dominant subject matter during our window is armed conflict, a domain in which
primary-source tooling substitutes for reporting.

The second feature connects to crisis informatics. Participants in online
discussion have been shown to assemble accounts of unfolding events from primary
sources, live tracking tools and archived material, a practice that operates
largely outside the institutional press \citep{starbird2014}. Live war mapping, vessel
tracking and flight tracking belong to that repertoire, and any audit that codes
sources only as news or not-news will record them as residue.

\subsection{Source taxonomies as instruments}

A final literature bears on the instrument rather than the object.
Substantive conclusions about the circulation of fake news depend materially on
which groundtruth labelling scheme a researcher adopts, as a comparison of
AllSides, Ad Fontes and Media Bias/Fact Check demonstrates \citep{bozarth2020}.
Comparable third-party labels anchor audits of partisan bias in search results
\citep{robertson2018}, which illustrates how widely the practice has been
adopted.

We take the point one step further. The rating databases that anchor this
practice are built around the American media market, and their coverage thins
sharply outside it. On a corpus whose dominant subject is conflict outside the
United States, that limitation becomes a measurement failure of the first order,
with consequences we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:coding}.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

The framework yields three propositions, each stated so that it can fail.

\textbf{H1 (counter-canon)} holds that if /pol/ maintains an alternative media
ecosystem, alternative outlets will be numerically substantial, mutually
co-cited and structurally central in the citation network.
Sections~\ref{sec:shares} and \ref{sec:network} test all three implications.
\textbf{H2 (infrastructure)} holds that if circulation is organised by platform
affordance and preservation need, platform and archive hosts will dominate the
citation supply, and co-citation communities will partition by subject matter in
place of editorial orientation. The same two sections test both parts.
\textbf{H3 (instrument dependence)} holds that if source taxonomies built for
the American media market are applied to an internationally sourced corpus,
measured news share will be biased downward by an amount large enough to alter
substantive conclusions, which Section~\ref{sec:coding} quantifies.

H1 and H2 are rivals over the same evidence, which is what makes the test
informative. H3 conditions both, since any estimate of news share is a joint
product of the board's behaviour and the taxonomy applied to it.

/pol/ in general or across time.

\subsection{Citation extraction}

The unit of analysis is the \textbf{citation event}: one external domain in one
post, deduplicated within the post. Links internal to 4chan are excluded.
Mobile and shortened hosts normalise to a canonical domain
(\texttt{youtu.be}, \texttt{m.youtube.com} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{youtube.com}),
and subdomain matching resolves cases such as \texttt{en.wikipedia.org}.

We flag one failure mode because it is a plausible silent defect in any such
pipeline. An earlier version of ours retained only the first domain per post.
Because /pol/ posts characteristically lead with a video or tweet and append a
source, this systematically discarded the appended source: alternative-media
citations were undercounted 3.6-fold. All figures here use per-citation counting.

\subsection{Source coding and the undercount it corrected}
\label{sec:coding}

Domains are coded on a medium-type axis into seven categories, namely mainstream,
alternative and state-controlled news, social platform, institutional reference,
archive/file host, and unclassified. Deterministic structural rules handle
platforms, \texttt{.gov}/\texttt{.mil}/\texttt{.edu} suffixes and the Wikimedia
family.

An initial pass using curated news lists derived from US-centric rating
databases left 47.2\% of citations unclassified and put news outlets at 4.6\%.
That figure was an artefact. The unclassified bucket contained
\texttt{dailymail.com}, \texttt{abc.net.au}, \texttt{independent.co.uk},
\texttt{itv.com}, \texttt{standard.co.uk}, \texttt{telegraph.co.uk},
\texttt{timesofisrael.com}, \texttt{aljazeera.com} and \texttt{pravda.com.ua}:
British, Australian, Israeli and Ukrainian outlets absent from lists built
around the American media market, on a corpus dominated by threads about
ongoing wars.

We therefore hand-coded the 200 most-cited unclassified domains, covering 74.9\%
of that bucket, and publish the result as a versioned codebook of 203 domains.
Four rules were fixed in advance and applied uniformly. \textbf{Aggregators}
such as \texttt{msn.com} and \texttt{yahoo.com} are coded by what they
redistribute, while search engines and link shorteners fall outside the news
categories altogether. \textbf{State-controlled} status takes ownership and
funding as its criterion, setting editorial independence aside, which groups Al
Jazeera with the BBC, ABC (Australia), PBS, NHK, Deutsche Welle, Anadolu,
Channel News Asia and Minnesota Public Radio, and moves the BBC (135 citations)
out of mainstream. \textbf{Opinion Substacks and blogs} count as alternative
media, since they produce editorial content of their own. \textbf{Campaign
sites} remain unclassified, while state media outlets stay state-controlled.

Manual coding raised news media from 4.6\% to \textbf{12.8\%} of citations, a
2.8-fold undercount by the automated pass, and cut the unclassified bucket from
47.2\% to 23.6\%.

\subsection{Why this paper contains no sentiment analysis}
\label{sec:nosentiment}

We scored every cited post with
\texttt{cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest}
\citep{barbieri2020,loureiro2022} and then excluded the results. The reasoning
is worth stating, because the measure is a standard one and its omission is
deliberate.

Three problems compound. \textbf{Construct}: the score is computed over the whole
post, so it captures the ambient tone of a message, not the author's stance
toward the source cited in it. Inspection of scored posts finds hostility
directed at reported events, at ethnic groups, and at other posters, all of it
attributed to whichever domain the post happened to link, including cases where
the source is cited approvingly as supporting evidence. \textbf{Domain shift}:
the model was trained on Twitter. /pol/'s irony reads literally (the most
positively-scored news post in the corpus is a sarcastic caption on a census
map, scored $+0.90$), and the slurs the board uses as ordinary register are read
as hostility regardless of the poster's actual attitude. \textbf{Truncation}:
scoring caps at 128 tokens, and 42.3\% of cited posts exceed it.

Any of these could be quantified with a hand-annotated validation sample, which
we have not produced. Publishing a headline conclusion on an uncalibrated
instrument seemed worse than publishing a narrower paper without one. The
scoring code remains in the repository behind a flag so the analysis can be
revisited after validation.

Everything reported below is derived from citation structure alone.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Proportions carry 95\% Wilson score intervals \citep{wilson1927}. Concentration
uses the Gini coefficient and a normalised Herfindahl--Hirschman index over
per-domain citation counts. The co-citation graph links domains appearing in the
same thread, keeping edges of weight $\geq 2$; communities come from Louvain,
and orientation sorting is measured by attribute assortativity
\citep{newman2003}. Because alternative domains are rarely cited and therefore
sparsely connected, the clustering test permutes labels only among nodes of
comparable degree, since a naive label permutation would credit them with
connectivity they do not have.

News media account for 12.8\% of citation events; platforms for 43.7\%; archives

Proportions are stable across the window with no trend: over the 24 captures,

\subsection{Concentration}

The diet is narrow. The domain distribution has Gini $0.821$ and normalised HHI
$0.091$. YouTube alone supplies \textbf{28.9\%} of citations; the top ten supply
49.1\% and the top fifty 69.0\%. Twelve domains account for half of all
citations, while 989 of 1{,}408 domains (70.2\%) appear exactly once
(Figure~\ref{fig:main}b).

\subsection{No alternative-media cluster}
\label{sec:network}

If /pol/ maintained a counter-canon, alternative outlets should travel together:
cited in the same threads as one another rather than alongside the established
press. They do not.

The co-citation graph has 286 nodes and 1{,}846 edges (density $0.045$), with
Louvain modularity $0.429$ over six communities. Assortativity by category over
the full graph is $+0.054$, so domains co-occur with little regard to what kind of
source they are.

Restricting to news domains sharpens the test. The news subgraph has 100 nodes
(68 mainstream, 20 alternative, 12 state-controlled) and 97 edges. Assortativity
by orientation is $\mathbf{-0.042}$: if anything slightly disassortative, and
indistinguishable from no sorting. Alternative outlets share exactly one edge
with another alternative outlet, against $0.81$ expected under a
degree-preserving null ($p=0.62$). They are also peripheral: mean degree $0.9$,
against $2.2$ for mainstream and $2.1$ for state-controlled. Most alternative
citations appear in threads where no other news source is cited at all.

The communities that do exist are topical. The largest (198 domains) is
general-purpose, anchored on YouTube, Wikipedia, the BBC and the Guardian, and
holds 59 mainstream, 14 alternative and 10 state-controlled domains
\emph{together in the same community}. The smaller ones are a war-tracking
cluster (\texttt{marinetraffic}, \texttt{flightradar24}, \texttt{flightaware},
\texttt{oilprice}), a US-politics platform cluster (\texttt{twitter},
\texttt{truthsocial}, \texttt{rumble}, \texttt{tiktok}), and a file-sharing
cluster (\texttt{files.catbox.moe}, \texttt{t.me}, \texttt{archive.today}).
Sourcing organises by subject matter and platform affordance, not by editorial
orientation.

\subsection{Archives and OSINT trackers}

Two behaviours emerge that a news-centric scheme would hide. Archives and file
hosts (\texttt{files.catbox.moe}, \texttt{rentry.org},
\texttt{archive.4plebs.org}, \texttt{archive.today}) take 11.0\% of citations:
hosting media outside platform moderation, and preserving content, including
4chan's own deleted threads via 4plebs, against removal.

Within the unclassified remainder, 571 citations (5.7\% of the corpus, 24.0\% of
that bucket) across 14 domains are live war-mapping and vehicle-tracking
services, namely \texttt{liveuamap}, \texttt{deepstatemap.live}, \texttt{marinetraffic},
\texttt{flightradar24}, \texttt{adsbexchange}. On a board whose largest
identifiable thread topic is armed conflict, primary-source OSINT tooling is
cited about half as often as all news media combined.

\subsection{A measured floor on survivorship}
\label{sec:deletion}

Sixty-two pairs of repeated captures of the same thread let us measure what is
usually asserted: \textbf{19 of 2{,}776 observed posts (0.68\%, 95\% CI
[0.44, 1.07])} had disappeared by the following capture, affecting 7 threads.

This must be read for what it is. It measures post-level deletion \emph{within
threads that survived to be captured twice}. It says nothing about threads
deleted entirely, nor about threads born and dying between two daily
captures, which at a daily cadence on a board with /pol/'s velocity is the
larger loss channel. It is a \textbf{floor on content loss} from a self-selected
sample, not an estimate of it. We report it because a measured floor is more
useful than an unmeasured caveat.

\section{Discussion}

The counter-canon is not there to be found. On this board, in this window,
alternative outlets are neither numerous (1.8\% of citations) nor connected to
one another (one co-citation edge among twenty domains) nor central (mean degree
below one). They appear as isolated citations inside threads organised around
topics, beside the mainstream outlets they are supposed to displace. Whatever
/pol/'s relationship to the legacy press, ``replaced by an alternative
ecosystem'' does not describe the citation structure.

The framing is also mis-scaled. A literature asking whether /pol/ prefers
alternative to mainstream media is asking about 10.1\% of what /pol/ circulates.
Platforms supply more than three times as many citations as all news combined,
and archives alone outnumber any single news category. The characteristic act on
this board is passing along a video, a screenshot, a mirror or a map, with the
article a comparatively rare object.

The measurement history carries its own lesson. Our first pass, using curated
lists derived from US-centric rating databases, put news at 4.6\% of citations,
and hand-coding put it at 12.8\%. The error had a direction, since it removed
British, Australian, Israeli and Ukrainian outlets from a corpus whose dominant
subject is war outside the United States. Any audit applying American media-bias
taxonomies to an internationally sourced corpus should expect an undercount of
this order and check for it directly.

A final word is owed on what these results do not reach. Nothing here speaks to
how /pol/ \emph{treats} the sources it cites. Citation is not endorsement, and on
a board whose characteristic mode is derision it may often be the opposite.
Establishing that would require the stance measurement this paper declines to
fake (Section~\ref{sec:nosentiment}), and it remains the obvious next study.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The codebook is the work of a single coder. The 203 domains were coded by one
author against fixed written rules, with 12 uncertain cases flagged. No second
coder annotated independently, so no Cohen's $\kappa$ or Krippendorff's $\alpha$
is reported. Structural categories (platform, archive, institutional) rest on
deterministic rules and are robust, while the news subdivisions rest on that
single judgment.

The network test is small. Twenty alternative domains and 97 news-subgraph edges
make a thin basis. The absence of clustering is consistent with the descriptive
picture and with the community structure, though a larger corpus could reveal
structure this one cannot resolve. We report it as evidence against the
counter-canon claim, stopping short of any proof that such clustering is
impossible.

Coding runs on a single axis. A fuller design codes each domain on three
orthogonal axes (medium type, editorial orientation, ownership) against external
databases, since forcing them into one label is what makes Fox News, BBC and
France 24 irreconcilable. We code medium type only, with ownership folded into
the state-controlled category by rule. The orientation test inherits that
coarseness, asking whether mainstream, alternative and state-controlled outlets
cluster, and leaving a finer left-right gradient untested.

A quarter of citations remain unclassified. Some 23.6\% fall outside all
categories, concentrated in a long tail of which 904 domains are cited exactly
once. Hand-coding covered 74.9\% of the original unclassified bucket, and the
remainder could contain further news outlets, which would raise the 12.8\%
figure again.

Survivorship bias is bounded below rather than corrected, as
Section~\ref{sec:deletion} sets out. Moderation removes the most extreme content
preferentially, so the surviving corpus is filtered in the direction of the
phenomenon studied. Source-distribution estimates are relatively robust, since
deletion would need to correlate with cited-source category.

The scope is one board, 24 daily captures and 45 days. Results describe the
surviving, publicly visible discourse of /pol/ during that window, and do not
generalise across time or to other imageboards.

\section{Conclusion}

/pol/'s information diet is not a media diet. News outlets of every political
description together supply an eighth of its citations, while platforms supply
more than four in ten and archives more than one in ten. The alternative press
that the board is supposed to have adopted in place of the legacy press is
rare, peripheral, and unclustered: its outlets do not travel together, they
travel beside the mainstream outlets they were supposed to replace, inside
threads organised by subject matter.

Two methodological results follow the substantive one. Source taxonomies built
for the American media market misdescribe an internationally-sourced corpus
badly enough to invert a headline figure. And a measure that cannot be validated
on its target domain is better excluded than caveated, since a narrower paper is
worth more than a broader one resting on an uncalibrated instrument. The
productive next step is the stance measurement this paper declines to attempt:
what /pol/ does with the sources it cites, as opposed to which ones it cites.
